A steel detailer needs approved design drawings, the project specification, clear connection requirements, interface information from other disciplines, the governing standards and drawing conventions, and agreed administration rules for numbering, software, formats and review. Missing inputs do not stop detailing outright. They turn into queries, assumptions and holds, and each one adds time and revision risk. Sending a complete package at the start is the cheapest schedule protection you can buy. If you are about to request quotations, pair this checklist with our guide to [writing an RFQ for a detailing package](/resources/how-to-write-an-rfq-for-structural-detailing).

## The checklist

### 1. Design information

- [ ] Structural design drawings at an agreed status (issued for construction, or frozen for the areas to be detailed)
- [ ] Plans, elevations and sections with grids, levels and member sizes
- [ ] Design model (IFC or native), if one exists, with its revision
- [ ] Member and connection forces or reactions, where the detailer is to select connections
- [ ] Camber, pre-set and special fabrication requirements

### 2. Specifications and materials

- [ ] Project structural steel specification
- [ ] Steel grades for profiles, plates and hollow sections
- [ ] Bolt type, grade, coating and tightening category
- [ ] Welding requirements and weld symbols convention
- [ ] Surface preparation, coating or galvanising system (this affects venting, drainage holes and slip factors)
- [ ] Fire protection allowances, if any

### 3. Connection responsibility and criteria

- [ ] A statement, for each connection type, of who designs it
- [ ] Standard connection details or design criteria for detailer-selected connections
- [ ] Any connections delegated to a fabricator's engineer, and how their calculations are submitted

Under ANSI/AISC 303-22, the owner's designated representative for design must indicate one of three connection options. Under BS EN 1090-2, comparable requirements belong in the execution specification. Whichever applies, it must be written down before modelling starts. Our article on [connection responsibility](/resources/structural-design-vs-detailing-responsibilities) explains the options.

### 4. Interfaces from other disciplines

- [ ] Piping loads, pipe support locations and penetrations (pipe racks, platforms)
- [ ] Vendor equipment drawings: footprints, anchor layouts, access and maintenance clearances
- [ ] Cable tray, ladder and instrument support requirements
- [ ] Foundation and anchor bolt drawings, or confirmation that the detailer prepares anchor plans
- [ ] Existing structure drawings or survey data for [brownfield tie-ins](/resources/brownfield-structural-modification-drawings)
- [ ] Architectural or cladding interfaces, where applicable

### 5. Standards and project conventions

- [ ] Governing standards and editions named in the specification (for example AISC 360/303, BS EN 1993/1090, or client standards)
- [ ] Execution class where BS EN 1090-2 applies. Clause 4.1.2 states "If no execution class is specified EXC2 shall apply", but the class should still be stated
- [ ] Client drawing standard: title block, scales, sheet sizes, layering
- [ ] Marking and numbering convention
- [ ] Tolerance requirements beyond the standard

### 6. Administration

- [ ] Software and version (e.g. Tekla Structures release), and model ownership at handover
- [ ] Exchange formats required (native, IFC, DWG, PDF, NC/DSTV)
- [ ] Model origin, coordinate system and units
- [ ] Review cycle: who comments, the turnaround time, and the comment format
- [ ] Issue milestones and priority areas
- [ ] Point of contact for technical queries

## What happens when inputs are missing

| Missing input | Typical consequence |
|---|---|
| Connection responsibility | Connections modelled and then rejected; rework across many assemblies |
| Coating system | Wrong hole sizes, no vent holes for galvanising, incorrect slip assumptions |
| Vendor drawings | Equipment supports held; platforms re-cut after the vendor's final data arrives |
| Piping supports | Secondary steel missing from the shop package; added later as site work |
| Drawing standard | Full re-dressing of sheets after the first issue |

## Starting with partial information

Real projects rarely provide everything on day one. A practical approach:

1. **Split the package** into areas that are frozen and areas that are not.
2. **Log assumptions** in writing, each with an owner and a closing date.
3. **Put holds on drawings** where information is pending, and show hold clouds on the issued sheets.
4. **Prioritise anchor bolt plans** and long-lead members.
5. **Re-baseline** when major inputs arrive, rather than patching piece by piece.

## How inputs are recorded

An input register lists every document received with its number, revision and date, and which areas of the model use it. When a revised input arrives, the register shows which drawings are affected. This is the basis for controlled revisions, and it is how {brand} structures each assignment: inputs are reviewed and gaps raised before production starts. ## Frequently asked questions

**Can detailing start before the design is issued for construction?**
Often yes, for frozen areas, with assumptions and holds recorded. The risk of rework is higher and should be priced and scheduled accordingly.

**Who supplies anchor bolt plans?**
It varies. Sometimes the civil design team does; sometimes the steel detailer does, from the steel base plate design. State it in the scope.

**What if the specification conflicts with the drawings?**
Raise an RFI. The order of precedence should be defined in the contract documents. The detailer should not choose between conflicting requirements.
